# Sharjah Tourism Innovation Hackathon 2026: event brief

Compiled 10 October 2026 from the organizer emails (Ali Reza Sajun, Lab Instructor, Department of Computer Science and Engineering, AUS), the Hackathon Guidelines, the Code of Conduct, the Agenda, and the Mentor Profiles deck. Where the documents disagree on wording, the stricter reading is kept.

## 1. The event in one paragraph

The American University of Sharjah (AUS) runs the hackathon on 10 and 11 October 2026 in the AUS Main Building, with the Department of Computer Science and Engineering and the School of Business Administration organizing and the Sharjah Commerce and Tourism Development Authority (SCTDA) as ecosystem partner. The challenge is open-ended within the theme "Innovating the Sustainable Future of Tourism" (the Guidelines phrase it "Sustainable Innovation in Tourism"). Mentors shortlist ten teams at midday on Day 2, and those teams pitch to a judging panel for five minutes plus three minutes of questions. The top three teams receive startup mentorship from Sheraa and the AUS ARC Research Center, and the winning team presents on the main stage at the Sharjah International Travel and Tourism Forum (SITTF) 2026, in addition to cash prizes.

## 4. Rules that can disqualify a team

1. Teams consist of the same four registered members. No substitutions, no additions, no incomplete teams.
2. All four members must be on site for the full duration and take part in all activities. Temporary absence for health or emergency reasons must be reported to the organizers in advance.
3. No external advisors, faculty, or guests may assist or be present, physically or remotely, during development or presentations.
4. All work must be produced during the hackathon. Arriving with a pre-existing project is not permitted. Existing ideas and open-source resources may be used if properly credited.
5. Plagiarism, presenting work that is not the team's own, or using others' ideas without consent is prohibited.
6. Only publicly available or provided datasets may be used unless explicit permission exists; no personal data may be collected or shared without consent; projects that promote harm, discrimination, or unethical use of technology are rejected.
7. Failure to disclose generative AI usage may cost points or lead to disqualification at the judges' discretion.

## 5. Generative AI usage policy

Generative AI tools (the Guidelines name ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, DALL-E, Midjourney, and Claude) are permitted. Before presenting, each team must upload an AI Usage Disclosure Document with its presentation materials. The document must state which tools were used, which specific components were generated (code, text, images, design concepts), and how the generated outputs were integrated or modified. The judges evaluate the team's originality, understanding, and technical contribution rather than the AI output itself, so the disclosure should show what the team decided, changed, and verified.

A running draft is kept in `03_AI_Usage_Disclosure.md` in this folder and must be updated whenever a new tool or generated asset enters the project.

## 6. Presentation and judging

Teams must submit their presentation materials (slides, video, demo link, or visuals) before the presentation period begins, at a time the organizers will announce. The format is free: slides, videos, mockups, live demos, campaign visuals, or any medium. The judges assess clarity, creativity, relevance, and overall impact regardless of format; the stated goal is to convey what was built or conceptualized, why it matters, and how it contributes to innovation in tourism. Combined with the AI policy, the criteria that matter are therefore: clarity, creativity, relevance to the theme, impact, originality, understanding, and technical contribution.

The organizing department's public hackathon page (auscse.com/hackathon, opened on Day 1) publishes the weights the judges use: startup viability and commercial feasibility 20%, potential impact and value creation 20%, innovative utilization of emerging technologies 20%, problem definition clarity 10%, relevance to the theme 10%, relevance to Sharjah and the UAE context 10%, and creativity and quality of presentation 10%. The same page lists the prize pool as AED 45,000 (first AED 20,000, second AED 15,000, third AED 10,000) and the accepted formats as a concept pitch, a working MVP or prototype, data visualizations, a short video pitch of one to three minutes, or UX and UI prototypes, used alone or combined. Commercial feasibility therefore weighs as much as the technology, which is the single most useful fact for shaping the pitch.

Projects may take any of these forms: technology-driven solutions (apps, platforms, data systems, prototypes), marketing campaigns or communication strategies, new tourism concepts, destinations, or experiences, and policy initiatives that improve the visitor journey or community impact.

## 6a. What the hosts said on stage (Day 1 slides)

The opening session slides add three things the written documents do not say.

Prize: the first prize is AED 20,000 (slide "Venture Stage and Rewards Trajectory"). The trajectory the hosts describe is hackathon sprint (problem definition and prototype), then incubation and pilot (customer validation, partner pilots in Sharjah), then commercial launch (expansion across the UAE).

MVP guidance (slide "The Minimum Viable Product"): test the most important assumption with real users. Avoid a full-featured tourism super-app, complex AI backend infrastructure, targeting multiple customer segments, and months of development before feedback. Start with a clickable, high-fidelity prototype that solves one specific visitor problem with one core feature, and test it early with real people. For Daleel this means one visitor problem, one core feature demonstrated end to end on a phone, and everything else shown as a roadmap.

Problem framing (slide "From Idea to Market"): map visitor friction across Sharjah destinations, naming Khorfakkan Beach, the Heart of Sharjah, and Al Noor Island as the key locations and discovery, mobility, and language barriers as the friction types; frame a value-driven problem statement before proposing technology; then apply AI, AR, or smart data as the enabler. The hosts' own worked example (slide "Designing the Value Proposition") was "Discover Sharjah AI": target customer families visiting Sharjah; unmet need that first-time visitors struggle to build personalized itineraries combining heritage and nature; solution a smart tourism assistant; value less planning time and richer personalized experiences. Because the organizers used an AI companion as their illustration, an AI guide on its own will not read as original; the pitch must lead with a sharper problem and a mechanism the example does not have.

## 8. Code of Conduct in brief

Respect and inclusion (no discrimination or harassment, no exclusionary language), collaboration and fair play (no plagiarism, registered members only, credit open source, respect intellectual property), professionalism (respect mentors, judges, sponsors, and organizers; follow timelines and safety guidance), environment (tidy workspace, no illegal substances), data and ethics (public or provided datasets only, no personal data without consent), consequences (removal, disqualification, report to the participant's university, escalation to campus authorities), and reporting (contact an organizer or volunteer; reports are handled confidentially).
